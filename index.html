How I do this, in detail

If Initiation is weak, everything after it wobbles: scope creeps, stakeholders disagree and requirements conflict. Even when leadership has already chosen the direction, the BA adds value by pinning down the problem, the impacts and measurable outcomes, so later decisions rest on evidence, not opinion.

  • Work in a deliberate order: stakeholders, then current state, then issues and opportunities, then the problem statement, and only then high-level requirements
  • Write the problem as problem, impact and outcome, backed by evidence, with no solution in it
  • Rate stakeholders on influence, interest and impact, not everyone as "high", and choose an engagement approach for each
  • Surface the trade-offs early, such as cost against capability, rather than hiding them or resolving them too soon
  • Play findings back to stakeholders before formalising, and log assumptions, open questions and decisions needed later

CRM transformation: The client had already shortlisted three CRMs. Before comparing them, I set out five evidenced business concerns and rated stakeholders in four distinct groups rather than marking everyone high, which surfaced an unconsulted technical owner as an integration risk. See it →

Radio Ramadan: Trello was already in use, so the question was how to make it work, not what to buy. I recorded which details stakeholders had confirmed, and kept a column of open questions to confirm against every user story. See it →

How I do this, in detail

If Initiation makes the problem clear, Planning makes the change clear. Stakeholder requirements aren't brainstormed; they come out of the gap analysis, the journey and the impact work. Skip that, and design becomes guesswork and operational impacts get missed.

  • Model the current processes in detail, including the manual hand-offs and workarounds
  • Set the future state beside the current state and name the gaps between them
  • Walk the future journey and ask, at each step, what must be true for it to work safely and well
  • Assess the impact on each group, such as customers, operations and compliance, before any design starts
  • Turn those findings into stakeholder requirements that trace back to the business need

Radio Ramadan: I kept the team's existing Trello stages and added only what the gaps called for, such as a brief-complete check and recorded approvals, so the change stayed proportionate to the problem. See it →

How I do this, in detail

Requirements that aren't grounded in an agreed process are guesswork, and they get rewritten every time a workshop changes something. I keep business rules separate because they change faster than capabilities: when a limit changes, one rule is updated, not every requirement.

  • Model the future-state processes first, including what happens when things fail
  • Analyse each step: what must the system do for this step to work?
  • Group those behaviours into functional requirements that can each be built and tested on their own
  • Keep business rules (thresholds, limits, policies) separate from the requirements they govern
  • Add non-functional requirements and wireframes, and work out what the organisation must do to be ready

How I do this, in detail

A story is only useful if a developer can build it and a tester can test it without needing a meeting. And a perfect build can still fail at go-live if the organisation isn't ready, so readiness is tracked from the first sprint, not the last.

  • Build the backlog from the design outputs, not from a blank page
  • Take the persona and outcome from the stakeholder need, and the capability from the grouped requirements
  • Write Given/When/Then acceptance criteria covering the happy path, business rules, failure paths, edge cases and side effects
  • Check stories are genuinely ready before sizing, and treat every developer question as a possible gap in the criteria
  • Track organisational readiness (communications, training, procedures) alongside the build, with owners and dates

UAT

Why it matters

Prove the business got what it asked for, with evidence rather than opinion.

How I do this, in detail

UAT is the business confirming that this is what it asked for. A weak UAT means a weak sign-off, and risk goes live with the solution. Because every test traces to a criterion, the testing is structured and defensible, not exploratory.

  • Plan UAT during design, starting with a test plan: scope, entry and exit criteria, roles and defect process
  • Derive every test case from an acceptance criterion and keep the link
  • Cover negative and boundary conditions, where the real defects tend to hide
  • Triage each defect: is it genuine, or a new requirement that belongs in change control?
  • Close with a summary report and written sign-off, with owners and dates for anything conditional

How I do this, in detail

Most BAs move on at go-live and never find out whether the project worked. Closing the loop answers the question the project started with. A measurement plan without named owners is just decoration.

  • Ask "how will we know this worked?" from Initiation onwards, not just at the end
  • Define each benefit with a baseline, a target, a measurement method, a data source, a named owner and a reporting cadence
  • Record results honestly: "on track" is not "achieved", and an early warning is a useful signal, not a failure
  • Hand over one to one, confirming each owner can actually get the data, not just that they agreed in a meeting
  • Account for every deliverable, and capture lessons that are specific enough to act on

Radio Ramadan: I closed the analysis without claiming any unmeasured benefits, proposed baseline measures for a pilot, and wrote lessons as actions, such as agreeing a business owner and the required fields before piloting. See it →

Traceability

Runs through every phase

My process

  1. 1Business need
  2. 2Stakeholder need
  3. 3Requirement
  4. 4User story
  5. 5Test
Why it matters

Every test proves a story, every story delivers a requirement, every requirement serves a business need.

TC-14traces to stories 2.6, 3.1 and 3.2 in Radio Ramadan

Evidence from my projects

Requirements traceabilityImpact analysisConsistent IDs
How I do this, in detail

Traceability proves that no objective was left unsupported and that no requirement is random. When something changes, I can see straight away what else it affects. At sign-off, I can show that every need was built and tested.

  • Keep one chain: business requirement, then stakeholder requirement, then functional requirement, then story, then test
  • Give every item a consistent ID and link each one to what it came from
  • Check for orphans: no requirement without a source, and no business need without coverage
  • Use the links to assess the impact of any change
